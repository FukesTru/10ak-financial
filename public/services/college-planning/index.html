College Planning for Families in Los Angeles

1Oak Financial Group is a tax, accounting and financial planning firm in Monrovia, California, helping families plan how to pay for college. We cover where to save, how financial aid looks at your income and savings, and which education tax breaks you may use, while keeping your retirement on track.

Where to begin

What should you do first when you start planning for college?

  1. List every account that could pay for school

    Write down each savings account, investment account and 529 plan, and who owns it. A 529 plan is an education savings account with tax breaks when the money goes to school costs. Who owns each account can change how financial aid treats it.

  2. Note your children's ages and school hopes

    The years left before enrollment shape how much to save each month. The kinds of schools your child likes matter too, since costs vary a lot between public and private colleges.

  3. Check where your retirement stands

    Look at your own retirement savings before you set a college target. Students can borrow for school, but no one lends you money for retirement.

  4. Put aid deadlines on your calendar

    The FAFSA is the federal form families file to apply for student aid, and it usually opens in the fall. Many colleges also set their own dates, and some come earlier. Start a list in your child's junior year of high school.

Situations we see

Which college planning situations do we see most?

Parents of young children

Your kids are in preschool or grade school. You want to start early and keep the monthly amount easy to manage.

Families with a high schooler

Time is short, and you need to understand the aid forms and how to use what you have saved. For each school year, the FAFSA generally uses family income from 2 calendar years earlier. Money choices made from about sophomore year of high school on can show up on aid forms.

Grandparents who want to help

You might open a 529 in your own name, pay tuition directly, or give money to the parents. Each choice affects aid and your own estate plans in a different way.

Families who expect little need-based aid

Your income likely rules out most need-based grants. The focus moves to saving in tax-friendly ways, merit scholarships and cash flow during the college years.

Parents paying private school tuition now

You are paying for private school today and saving for college too. We look at whether to use 529 money for both, and what the state tax cost would be.

How we help

How does 1Oak help you plan for college costs?

Paying for college touches tax law, aid formulas and each school's own policies. Families sometimes save in the wrong account, or in the right account under the wrong name, and only find out when the aid offer arrives. We help you set a plan early and adjust it as your child grows.

Because we also do tax planning, we can line up education savings with the rest of your taxes. That includes credits you may claim once tuition bills arrive. We also check that your life insurance would keep the plan going if a parent died before the kids finish school.

  • 529 plan guidance

    Earnings in a 529 plan come out free of federal income tax when spent on qualified college costs. Those include tuition, fees and books, plus room and board for students enrolled at least half time. We explain how plans differ on fees, investment choices and ownership rules.

  • A savings target you can keep up

    We estimate what the schools you are considering may cost by the time your child starts. Then we set a monthly or yearly amount that fits your budget and revisit it as the start date nears.

  • FAFSA and the Student Aid Index

    The FAFSA produces your Student Aid Index. The Student Aid Index is a number colleges use to figure how much need-based aid you may qualify for. We explain how it is figured, and how the timing of income and the owner of each asset can change it.

  • The CSS Profile

    Many private colleges also ask for the CSS Profile, a second aid form with its own formula. It can count things the FAFSA leaves out. At some schools, that includes your home equity, which is your home's value minus what you owe on it.

  • Scholarships and net price

    Merit scholarships reward grades, talent or other achievements. Need-based aid depends on family finances. Net price is what you pay after grants and scholarships. Knowing which aid your child may get helps you build a college list with a net price that fits.

  • Education tax credits

    A tax credit lowers your tax bill dollar for dollar. The American Opportunity credit and the Lifetime Learning credit can offset part of what you pay for school. Both shrink and then stop as income rises past limits set by law. You also cannot claim a credit on costs paid with tax-free 529 money.

California rules

How do California rules affect college savings?

California gives no state income tax deduction for money you put into a 529 plan. You still get the federal tax-free growth, and you can generally use a plan from any state. So we compare plans mainly on fees and features.

Federal law allows some 529 withdrawals for K-12 tuition, meaning private elementary, middle or high school. California does not follow that rule. On your state return, the earnings part of those withdrawals may be taxed, plus an extra state tax. We check that cost before you use college savings for private school.

If your child does not go to college, you still have choices. You can change the beneficiary, the person the account is for, to another eligible family member, usually without tax. A non-qualified withdrawal is money taken out for something other than school costs. Its earnings are taxed as income, with a 10% federal penalty plus an extra California tax. Some exceptions to the penalty apply, such as when your child earns a tax-free scholarship.

A newer federal rule allows a limited rollover from a 529 to a Roth IRA in the child's name. A Roth IRA is a personal retirement account, and its growth can be taken out tax-free once its rules are met. The 529 must have been open for many years, and yearly and lifetime caps apply. California may not treat these rollovers the same way, so we confirm the state rules first.

Questions

What do people ask about college planning?

How early should we start saving for college?

As early as you comfortably can, often in the first few years of a child's life. Time gives each deposit more room to grow. Starting later still helps, since a plan made in high school can shape aid forms and how much you borrow.

How does our income affect financial aid?

Income is usually the biggest factor in your Student Aid Index. Parent assets, including a parent-owned 529 plan, count too, but at a much lower rate than income. Retirement accounts generally are not reported as assets on the FAFSA. Families with higher incomes may still get aid at high-cost schools, so it is worth running the numbers.

What happens if my child does not go to college?

You can name a sibling, cousin or other eligible relative as the new beneficiary. You can also take the money out, but the earnings are taxed, with a 10% federal penalty and an extra California tax. A limited rollover to your child's Roth IRA may also be possible if the account meets the conditions.

Should grandparents open their own 529 plan?

It can work well. Under the current FAFSA, money from a grandparent-owned 529 no longer counts as student income. Some colleges that use the CSS Profile may still ask about these accounts. Grandparents should also think about how the gifts fit their own estate plans.

Can we claim an education credit and use a 529 in the same year?

Often yes, as long as you do not count the same dollars twice. Costs used for the American Opportunity or Lifetime Learning credit cannot also be paid with tax-free 529 earnings. Some families pay part of the bill from income to claim a credit and use the 529 for the rest. Both then need to be reported correctly on your tax return.

Should we save for college or retirement first?

For most families, retirement comes first when money is tight. Your child can draw on loans and aid, and you will not have those options for retirement. We often fund retirement, save what you can for college, and revisit the split as income grows.
