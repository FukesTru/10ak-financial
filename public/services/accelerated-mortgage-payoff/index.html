Accelerated Mortgage Payoff Planning in Los Angeles

1Oak Financial Group is a tax, accounting and financial planning firm in Monrovia, California, with a review that shows whether paying off your home loan early makes sense. If it does, we help you pick a method that fits your cash flow, your taxes and your other goals.

Where to begin

What should you do first if you want to pay off your mortgage faster?

  1. Read your latest mortgage statement

    Find your balance, rate, remaining term and monthly payment. Look for any prepayment penalty, which is a fee some loans charge if you pay early. Many loans have none, but check yours.

  2. Build your emergency savings first

    Keep several months of costs in cash before you send extra to the lender. Money paid into your home is hard to get back without selling or borrowing.

  3. Pay down higher-rate debt and get your full employer match

    Credit cards and other higher-rate loans usually come before extra mortgage payments. Also put in enough to get your full employer match. That is the money your employer adds to your retirement plan when you contribute.

  4. Ask how your servicer applies extra payments

    Your servicer is the company that collects your mortgage payment. Principal is the amount you still owe, not counting interest. Ask how to mark extra money as principal so it lowers your balance right away.

How we help

How does 1Oak help you decide on a faster mortgage payoff?

Owning your home outright lowers your monthly costs and can be a relief, especially in retirement. Still, each extra dollar sent to the lender is a dollar you cannot use for savings or investing. We weigh both sides with your real numbers.

Sometimes the numbers favor paying faster. Other times the same money may do more in accounts with tax breaks, and we will say so. If a refinance is also on your mind, we tie this work to our mortgage loan planning.

Here is a made-up example, with rounded figures that leave out taxes, insurance and fees. A $600,000, 30-year loan with a fixed rate of 6.25% has a principal and interest payment of about $3,694 a month. Over 30 years, that comes to about $729,900 of interest. Paying $500 extra a month from the start pays it off in about 22 years and avoids about $226,500 of interest.

Now take the same made-up loan at 3%. The principal and interest payment is about $2,530 a month, and total interest is about $310,700. The same $500 extra each month avoids only about $81,400 of interest. A lower rate shrinks the benefit of paying early, which is why investing the extra money may come out ahead in some cases.

  • Extra principal each month

    Adding a set amount to each payment and marking it as principal shortens the loan and cuts total interest. We check that your servicer applies it to the balance and does not hold it as an early payment of next month's bill.

  • Biweekly payments

    Paying half your monthly payment every 2 weeks adds up to 26 half payments a year. That equals 13 full payments instead of 12, or one extra payment each year. Adding 1/12 of your payment to principal each month gets about the same result.

  • Lump sums and recasting

    A bonus, business payout or inheritance can go straight to principal. Some lenders will then recast the loan. A recast keeps your rate and end date but lowers your monthly payment to match the smaller balance, usually for a fee. Not every loan allows it.

  • A shorter loan term

    Refinancing means taking out a new loan to pay off the old one. Moving from a 30-year loan to a 15- or 20-year loan this way often brings a lower rate and much less total interest. The required payment is higher, and there are new closing costs. We weigh that against the room in your monthly budget.

  • Home equity line plans

    Some plans run your paychecks through a home equity line of credit, a revolving loan backed by your home. They depend on a variable rate, steady extra cash and firm spending habits. If rates rise or spending grows, what you owe can go up. We explain how these plans work and their risks before you decide.

  • Paying down versus investing

    We compare your after-tax mortgage rate with a range of after-tax returns you might earn elsewhere. Your after-tax rate is your loan rate after any tax savings from deducting the interest. We also weigh cash access, risk and how you feel about debt. Returns are never certain, so we show several outcomes.

Situations we see

Which mortgage payoff situations do we see most?

Loans taken out at higher rates

Each extra dollar avoids interest at your loan's rate. That is a known number, and some people prefer it to an uncertain market return.

Pre-retirees who want lower fixed costs

You want the house paid off around the time paychecks stop. We look at how to get there without using up the savings you will live on.

Business owners with uneven income

Distributions and bonuses arrive at odd times. We help you set a simple rule for which windfalls go to the loan and which stay in reserve for slow months.

Couples who see debt differently

One of you wants the loan gone and the other would rather invest. Seeing the trade-offs with real numbers can make that talk easier.

Homeowners with a low rate from past years

Your loan rate is low compared with what savings may earn today. Paying early may still feel right to you, and that can be a reasonable choice when you understand the trade-off.

California rules

How do California rules affect paying off a mortgage early?

If you itemize, some mortgage interest may be deductible. Itemizing means listing your deductions one by one instead of taking the standard deduction, a flat amount set by law. Federal and California limits on the interest deduction are not the same. If you take the standard deduction, as many households now do, paying early avoids interest at your full loan rate.

Some people think about using retirement money to clear the loan. A pre-tax account is one where you got a tax break when you put money in. Withdrawals from it are usually taxed as income by the IRS and by California. Before age 59½, a withdrawal may also bring a 10% federal penalty unless an exception applies, and California adds its own extra tax. A large withdrawal in one year can also push you into a higher tax bracket, so each added dollar is taxed at a higher rate.

Paying off the loan does not change your California property tax. That tax is based mainly on your home's assessed value, the value the county uses to set the bill. You still owe it once the loan is gone. Your servicer may pay it today through escrow, an account funded by part of your monthly payment that covers property tax and insurance. After payoff, you pay those bills yourself, so we build them into your budget.

Questions

What do people ask about accelerated mortgage payoff?

Is paying off my mortgage early a good idea?

It can be, especially when your rate is fairly high, your emergency savings are solid and you carry no higher-rate debt. It is usually a lower priority if you will need the cash soon or your loan has a prepayment penalty. The answer depends on your rate, your taxes, your comfort with risk and how much being debt-free matters to you.

How much interest could I save by paying extra?

That depends on your balance, rate, remaining term and how much extra you pay. In the made-up example on this page, $500 extra a month avoids about $226,500 of interest at 6.25% and about $81,400 at 3%. Those figures only show how the math works. We model your own loan before you decide.

What if my mortgage rate is already low?

Then the case for paying early is usually weaker. If your after-tax rate is below what you could reasonably earn elsewhere after tax, extra money may do more in retirement accounts or savings. Returns are never certain, though. Some people still prepay for the comfort of owning the home outright, and that is a fair choice.

Do biweekly payment programs charge fees?

Some outside programs charge setup or per-payment fees. Some also hold your half payments and send them to the lender only once a month, which can reduce the benefit. Adding 1/12 of your payment to principal each month can get a similar result without a program fee. Ask your servicer how it handles extra payments before you sign up.

Should I use retirement money to pay off my house?

It is often costly. Pre-tax withdrawals are taxed as income, and early ones can bring federal and California penalties. Savings beyond your emergency fund may be a better source. We compare the after-tax cost of the withdrawal with the interest you would avoid.

Can I get money back out of my house later?

Not easily. Home equity is the share of your home's value that you own outright, and reaching it usually means selling, refinancing or borrowing against the home. Lenders may say no when you need it most, such as after a job loss or a slow year in your business. That is why we build cash reserves before speeding up a payoff.
