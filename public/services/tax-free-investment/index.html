Tax-Free Investment Planning for Los Angeles Households

1Oak Financial Group is a tax, accounting and financial planning firm in Monrovia, California, showing you how your savings may grow with less tax. We explain which accounts fit your income, what you may pay now or later, and the conditions that sit behind the word tax-free.

Where to begin

What should you do first before choosing a tax-free investment?

  1. Find your last 2 tax returns

    Your returns show your tax bracket and where your income comes from. A tax bracket is the rate that applies to the last dollars you earn. Knowing yours tells us which ideas are worth testing.

  2. List each account and how it is taxed

    Write down every account you own and what kind it is. Note whether it is taxed each year, taxed when money comes out, or set up to be tax-free later. Include old workplace plans from past jobs.

  3. Think about when you will need the money

    Many tax-free accounts come with age rules and waiting periods. Money you may need in the next few years often belongs somewhere easier to reach. Note any large costs ahead, like a home or a child's tuition.

  4. Tell us if you might leave California

    The state you live in when income is taxed can change the result. If you may retire to another state, say so early. It can affect the year it makes sense to pay tax.

How we help

How does 1Oak help with tax-free investment planning?

Tax-free usually means tax-free if you follow certain rules. A Roth account is a good example. It is a retirement account you fund with money you have already paid tax on. Growth can later come out with no tax once you meet age and timing rules, so you pay the tax up front and owe none on those withdrawals.

We explain these tools in plain terms and test them with your own numbers. We start from your tax return, so your investing and your tax planning use the same facts. This page explains account types and tax rules. It does not recommend any specific fund, bond or policy.

  • Roth IRA and Roth 401(k) choices

    An IRA is an individual retirement account you open on your own, and a 401(k) is a retirement plan offered through work. Both can come in a Roth version. Qualified withdrawals, meaning ones that meet the age 59½ and 5-year rules, come out tax-free. Direct Roth IRA deposits shrink and then stop above income limits that change each year.

  • Roth conversions

    A Roth conversion is when you shift savings out of a pre-tax account, such as a traditional IRA, and into a Roth IRA. Pre-tax means you got a tax break when the money went in. The amount you shift counts as taxable income that year. We test different amounts year by year, often in years when your income dips.

  • Backdoor Roth deposits

    If your income is too high to put money straight into a Roth IRA, there may be another path. You put after-tax money into a traditional IRA, then convert it. People call this a backdoor Roth. The pro-rata rule says that if you hold other pre-tax IRA money, part of the conversion is taxed, so we check every IRA first.

  • Municipal bond review

    A municipal bond is a loan you make to a state, city or public agency in return for interest. That interest is often free of federal income tax. We compare what a municipal bond pays after tax with what a taxable bond pays after tax. We also flag bonds that can count toward the alternative minimum tax, a second tax calculation that takes back some tax breaks.

  • Asset location

    Asset location means choosing which account holds which investment. Holdings that pay a lot of interest often fit better in tax-deferred accounts, where you owe no tax on growth until money comes out. Holdings you expect to grow the most may fit better in a Roth account. The right mix depends on your situation.

  • Tax-loss harvesting

    Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that has dropped in value so the loss can offset gains. Losses can also offset a limited amount of other income each year, and unused losses carry forward. The wash-sale rule blocks the loss for now if you buy the same or a nearly identical investment within 30 days before or after the sale.

Situations we see

Which tax-free investment situations do we see most?

High earners over the Roth IRA income limit

Your income may be too high for direct Roth IRA deposits. A Roth option in your workplace plan, a backdoor Roth or a conversion may still be open to you.

Early retirees with a few low-income years

The years between your last paycheck and your first Social Security check can have lower taxable income. Some people convert pre-tax savings in measured amounts during that time. This often happens before required minimum distributions begin. Those are yearly withdrawals the IRS requires from most pre-tax accounts after a set age.

Business owners with income that rises and falls

A slow year may be a sensible time to convert. A strong year may favor larger pre-tax savings, such as a cash balance plan. We look at both choices before the year closes.

Large cash or bond balances in a regular account

Interest from savings and taxable bonds is taxed every year, by the IRS and by California. We look at whether holding some of it in a different account, or in different bonds, could lower that yearly tax.

Parents and grandparents thinking about heirs

The original owner of a Roth IRA has no required minimum distributions. Heirs can generally take inherited Roth money out tax-free, within the timing rules that apply to them. That can make a Roth useful for money you plan to pass on.

Questions about life insurance sold as tax-free

Permanent life insurance is coverage meant to last your whole life, and it builds a cash value you can borrow against. That cash value grows tax-deferred, and policy loans are usually not taxed while the policy stays in force. Costs are high in the early years. A policy that lapses with a loan can bring a tax bill, and one funded too heavily can lose some tax breaks.

California rules

How do California rules affect tax-free investing?

California taxes most investment income as regular income, including capital gains. Capital gains are profits from selling an investment for more than you paid. So interest and gains are often taxed by both the IRS and the state. For many households here, choosing the right account matters more as a result.

Bonds follow their own state rules. Interest on California municipal bonds is generally exempt from California income tax for residents. Interest on bonds from other states is generally taxed by California, even when the IRS does not tax it. With a bond fund, the state tax depends on what the fund holds and how it reports its income.

California does not follow the federal tax breaks for health savings accounts. A health savings account, or HSA, is an account for medical costs that goes with a high-deductible health plan. Federal law lets HSA money go in, grow and come out for medical costs with no tax. California taxes the money you put in and the earnings each year.

If you convert to a Roth while you live here, California taxes the converted amount too. The state also gives no deduction for deposits into a 529 plan, which is a savings account for school costs with its own tax breaks. Our college planning page covers those accounts in more depth.

Questions

What do people ask about tax-free investment?

What does tax-free really mean for an investment?

It usually means tax-free if you meet certain conditions. With a Roth account, you pay tax before the money goes in, and growth comes out tax-free only after age and timing rules are met. Municipal bond interest may avoid federal tax, yet a gain when you sell can still be taxed. Tax-exempt interest can also count in the formulas for Medicare premiums and for how much of your Social Security is taxed.

Is a Roth conversion a good idea for me?

It may make sense if your tax rate today is lower than you expect it to be later. It also helps if you can pay the tax from savings outside the retirement account and leave the money invested for many years. It may make less sense if you expect a lower rate in retirement or need the money soon. We run your own numbers before suggesting any amount.

Can I undo a Roth conversion if the market drops?

No. Since 2018, a Roth conversion cannot be reversed, so the tax is owed even if values fall right after. Conversion income can also raise your Medicare premiums about 2 years later and make more of your Social Security taxable. For these reasons, we often look at converting in smaller amounts over several years.

How do I know if municipal bonds fit my situation?

Compare what a municipal bond pays with what a taxable bond of similar quality would pay after your federal and California taxes. Municipal bonds tend to look more useful as your tax rate rises. They usually make little sense inside an IRA, where the tax break goes unused. Also weigh how likely the issuer is to repay you and how long until the bond comes due. How easy it is to sell matters too.

Can a high earner still put money into a Roth?

Often yes. Many workplace plans offer a Roth option with no income limit, and a backdoor Roth or a conversion may also work. If you are under 59½, each conversion has its own 5-year wait before you can take out the converted amount without a penalty. Bring your latest return when we meet, and we will check current limits and your plan's options.

What are the risks of tax-free investing?

Tax laws can change, so a plan built on today's rules may work differently later. Bond prices usually fall when interest rates rise, and an issuer can run into money trouble. Owning mostly California bonds ties you to one state's finances. Any investment can lose value, no matter which account holds it.
