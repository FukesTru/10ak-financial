Advanced Financial Ratio Analysis for Business Owners in Los Angeles

1Oak Financial Group is a tax, accounting and financial planning firm in Monrovia, California, that turns your financial statements into a short set of ratios. A ratio compares 2 numbers, such as debt and earnings. Together they show how fast customers pay, how heavy your debt is and how much of each sales dollar becomes profit.

Where to begin

What should you gather first for a ratio review?

  1. Pull 2 to 3 years of statements

    Gather year-end balance sheets, profit and loss statements and tax returns. A balance sheet lists what the business owns and owes on a given date. A profit and loss statement shows sales, costs and profit over a period of time.

  2. Add this year's numbers

    Include the latest monthly or quarterly statements, so the review shows where you stand now.

  3. List every loan

    Write down each loan with its balance, rate and payment. Add any conditions the lender asks you to meet and report on.

  4. Sum up the household side

    A simple list of personal accounts, property and debts is enough to start. If you are not sure your records are ready, ask us before you gather anything.

How we help

How does 1Oak turn your statements into useful measures?

Most owners see a profit and loss statement every month and a balance sheet far less often. Ratios link them. Tracked over time, they can show a problem or an opportunity before it reaches the bank balance.

We pick the measures that fit how your business makes money and compare them with your own past results. Then we explain what each one suggests in plain English. Because much of an owner's wealth is often tied up in the company, we look at the household too. The work is only as reliable as the books behind it, which is where accounting and bookkeeping comes in.

  • Liquidity

    Liquidity is how easily you can pay the bills that are due soon. The current ratio compares short-term assets with bills due within a year. The quick ratio does the same but leaves out inventory.

  • Leverage

    Leverage is how much of the business is paid for with borrowed money. Debt-to-equity compares what you owe lenders with what the owners have put in and kept in the business.

  • Profit margins and returns

    Gross margin is what is left of sales after the direct cost of what you sell. Net margin is what is left after every cost. Return on assets shows how much profit the business earns on what it owns.

  • How cash moves through the business

    Days sales outstanding is the average number of days customers take to pay you. Inventory turnover counts how many times your stock sells and is replaced in a year. We also track how long you take to pay suppliers.

  • Debt coverage

    The debt service coverage ratio compares the cash flow you have available with the loan payments you must make. Lenders often write a minimum level into the loan as a covenant, which is a condition you agree to meet.

  • Household measures

    For owners, we add savings rate, debt-to-income, months of cash on hand and the share of net worth tied up in the business. Debt-to-income compares your monthly debt payments with your monthly income. Net worth is what you own minus what you owe.

Situations we see

Which ratio questions do owners bring us most?

Profitable on paper but short on cash

A made-up example. A company takes 52 days on average to collect from customers, holds inventory for about 70 days and pays suppliers in 28 days. That means its cash is tied up for about 94 days before it comes back. Collecting a week faster could free up cash without a new loan.

Bills due soon and thin reserves

Another made-up example. A company with a current ratio of 1.3 has $1.30 in short-term assets for each $1 of bills due within a year. If much of that is inventory, its quick ratio may fall below 1, and a slow month could squeeze it.

Getting ready for a new loan

Owners heading into a bank meeting want to see their numbers the way a lender will. In a made-up case, a debt service coverage ratio of 1.4 means $1.40 of available cash flow for each $1 of loan payments. We look at how much room that leaves before a loan condition comes into play.

Margins slipping over time

Sales may be up while profit stays flat. A falling gross margin can point to supplier costs that rose faster than your prices.

A household tied to a single business

When the company or its building makes up most of what you own, a slow year can hit both at once. Our strategic risk management work looks at that exposure more closely.

California rules

How do California and federal tax rules shape your ratios?

Ratios come from your financial statements, and those can differ from your tax returns. Federal and California tax rules allow different write-offs for equipment and buildings, and neither may match how your books record them. We note where each number comes from so the ratios compare like with like.

Before we calculate, we adjust for one-time items and for owner expenses run through the business. This brings the results closer to how the company really operates. Lenders often make their own adjustments, and each one sets its own standards.

On the personal side, mortgage lenders look closely at debt-to-income when you apply for a home loan. Limits vary by lender and loan program, and they can change. We do not lend money, but we can help you see where you stand before you apply.

Questions

What do people ask about advanced financial ratio analysis?

Which ratios matter most for my business?

It depends on how your business makes money. A distributor often watches inventory turnover and gross margin, while a professional firm focuses on collections and revenue per employee. A company with real estate or equipment loans pays close attention to debt service coverage.

How often should ratios be reviewed?

Quarterly works for many owner-led businesses, with a deeper look once a year alongside tax work. Companies with loan covenants, fast growth or tight cash may want to track a few measures monthly. Ongoing tracking can become part of our CFO advisory work.

Will this help me qualify for a business loan?

It can make you a better-prepared borrower, but no analysis can promise a yes or set the terms. Knowing your coverage, leverage and liquidity before you apply lets you fix weak spots and explain one-time items. Each lender uses its own standards.

Can you tell me what a normal ratio is for my industry?

Healthy ranges vary widely by industry, business model and company size, so a single outside number can mislead. We compare your ratios mainly with your own history. Published industry data, when reliable figures exist, serves only as loose context.

What happens if we fall outside a loan covenant?

It can lead to fees, tighter terms or, in some cases, a default, even when every payment was made on time. A default lets the lender use the remedies written into the loan agreement. We help you track covenant ratios between reporting dates so there are fewer surprises.

Is ratio analysis the same as valuing my business?

A business valuation is a separate job, usually done by a qualified appraiser. Ratio analysis shows the measures buyers tend to study, such as margins, debt and working capital, which is short-term assets minus short-term debts. Looking at these a few years before a sale gives you time to improve what you can.
